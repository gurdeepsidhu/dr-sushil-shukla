Understanding Congenital Heart Defects in Newborns

Written by Dr. Sushil Shukla • Dr. Sushil Shukla

Congenital Heart Defects (CHD) are structural heart issues present at birth. They can range from simple "holes in the heart" (like Atrial Septal Defects or Patent Ductus Arteriosus) to complex structural anomalies that restrict blood flow to the lungs or body.

Early identification through fetal echocardiography during pregnancy or clinical evaluation immediately after birth is vital. Today, advanced transcatheter interventions allow us to close defects like ASDs and PDAs using custom closing devices threaded through blood vessels, completely avoiding the need for open-heart surgery.

Heart Murmur in Children: Parents Ask, Dr. Sushil Shukla Answers

Written by Dr. Sushil Shukla, Pediatric Cardiologist

When parents are told that their child has a “heart murmur,” the first reaction is often worry. But a heart murmur does not necessarily mean that something is wrong with the heart.

To understand what a heart murmur really means, we asked Dr. Sushil Shukla, Pediatric Cardiologist, some of the most common questions parents have.

Parent: Doctor, what exactly is a heart murmur?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

A heart murmur is an additional sound heard when a doctor listens to the heart with a stethoscope.

Importantly, a heart murmur is a finding, not a diagnosis of heart disease.

Many healthy children have what we call an innocent or functional murmur. These murmurs are produced by normal blood flow through a structurally normal heart and are particularly common during childhood.

However, a murmur can sometimes be caused by a structural or other heart problem. Therefore, we assess the murmur together with the child's symptoms, examination findings and overall health.

Parent: Does every child with a murmur have a hole in the heart?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

No, absolutely not.

This is one of the most common misconceptions among parents.

A murmur may be present even when the heart is completely normal. Conversely, congenital heart conditions—including holes in the heart, valve abnormalities and other structural problems—can also produce murmurs.

So, hearing a murmur is a reason for appropriate assessment, not a reason to panic.

Parent: How do you decide whether a murmur is innocent or abnormal?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

We look at much more than just the presence of a murmur.

I consider the child's age, symptoms, growth, oxygen saturation, pulses and blood pressure, along with the location, intensity, timing and other characteristics of the murmur.

In many children, the history and clinical examination are reassuring enough to identify a typical innocent murmur.

If there is any concern about an underlying heart condition, further evaluation—particularly an echocardiogram (Echo)—may be recommended. An ECG may also be useful in selected situations.

Parent: What symptoms should parents watch for?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Most children with innocent murmurs have no symptoms at all.

However, a child with a murmur should be evaluated carefully if there are symptoms such as:

  • Breathlessness or unusually fast breathing
  • Difficulty feeding or excessive sweating during feeds, particularly in infants
  • Poor weight gain or poor growth
  • Bluish discoloration of the lips, tongue or inside of the mouth
  • Unusual tiredness
  • Reduced ability to exercise or difficulty keeping up with other children
  • Palpitations
  • Chest pain during physical activity
  • Dizziness or fainting, particularly during exercise

These symptoms do not automatically mean that a child has heart disease, but they deserve proper medical assessment.

Parent: Does every child with a heart murmur need an Echo?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Not necessarily.

An echocardiogram is an extremely useful test, but every innocent-sounding murmur does not automatically require one.

A pediatric cardiologist can first assess the child's history and perform a detailed cardiovascular examination. Based on these findings, we can decide whether an Echo is necessary.

When required, an echocardiogram is a safe, painless ultrasound examination that allows us to assess the heart chambers, valves, major blood vessels and blood flow without radiation.

Parent: My child is otherwise completely normal. Should I still be worried?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

If your child is active, growing normally and has no concerning symptoms, there is usually no reason to become frightened simply because a murmur has been heard.

In fact, innocent murmurs are very common in otherwise healthy children.

The important step is to determine whether the murmur has the characteristics of an innocent murmur or whether further evaluation is required.

When should a murmur receive prompt attention?

A murmur deserves prompt medical assessment when it is associated with bluish discoloration, low oxygen saturation, significant breathing difficulty, poor feeding or growth, abnormal pulses, exertional chest pain, fainting during exercise, or significant exercise intolerance.

A murmur detected in a newborn or young infant also deserves particularly careful assessment.

Dr. Sushil Shukla's Message to Parents

A heart murmur is a finding, not a diagnosis.

Many heart murmurs heard during childhood are completely innocent and occur in children with structurally normal hearts. However, a murmur can occasionally be the first clue to congenital or acquired heart disease.

My advice to parents is simple:

Don't panic—but don't ignore a persistent or unexplained heart murmur either.

A careful pediatric cardiac evaluation can usually determine whether the murmur is innocent or whether further investigation is required, giving families the clarity and reassurance they need.

This article is intended for general educational and awareness purposes only. It does not replace an individual medical consultation, examination or diagnosis.

VSD in Babies: Is Every “Hole in the Heart” Dangerous? Parents Ask, Dr. Sushil Shukla Answers

Written by Dr. Sushil Shukla, Pediatric Cardiologist

The words “hole in the heart” can immediately make parents anxious.

But does every hole require treatment? Can it close on its own? And what does a VSD mean for a baby's growth and future?

We asked Dr. Sushil Shukla, Pediatric Cardiologist, some of the most common questions parents ask after their baby is diagnosed with a VSD.

Parent: Doctor, what exactly is a VSD?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

VSD stands for Ventricular Septal Defect.

The two lower chambers of the heart—the right and left ventricles—are separated by a wall called the ventricular septum. An opening in this wall is called a VSD.

After birth, pressure is normally higher in the left ventricle than in the right ventricle. Therefore, in most VSDs, blood passes from the left side of the heart to the right side and then travels back towards the lungs.

The effect of a VSD depends on several factors, including its size, location, restrictiveness, the amount of blood passing through it, pulmonary artery pressure, and its effect on the heart chambers.

This is why two babies who both have a VSD may have very different clinical situations.

Parent: Is every VSD dangerous?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

No.

This is one of the most important things for parents to understand.

Many VSDs are small and cause little or no effect on the heart. Some become smaller or close spontaneously as the child grows.

A large VSD, however, can allow excessive blood flow to the lungs and increase the workload on the heart. Over time, an untreated significant VSD can also lead to high pressure in the lung circulation.

So, when I evaluate a baby with a VSD, I don't simply ask, “Is there a hole?”

I want to know: How large is it? Where is it located? How much blood is passing through it? Is the heart becoming enlarged? What is the pressure in the lung circulation? And how is the baby growing?

These factors determine how important the VSD actually is.

Parent: What symptoms can a baby with VSD have?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

A baby with a small VSD may have no symptoms at all and may grow completely normally.

Babies with a significant VSD may develop symptoms such as:

  • Fast or laboured breathing
  • Difficulty feeding
  • Excessive sweating during feeds
  • Taking a long time to finish feeds
  • Frequent pauses or breaks while feeding
  • Poor weight gain
  • Tiring easily during feeding
  • Recurrent respiratory illnesses in some children

Interestingly, babies with a large VSD may appear relatively well immediately after birth. Symptoms can become more noticeable during the following weeks as the resistance in the lung circulation naturally falls and more blood begins to pass through the VSD.

Parent: Can a VSD close by itself?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Yes. Many VSDs can become smaller, and some close spontaneously.

The likelihood of spontaneous closure depends particularly on the type, location and size of the VSD.

Small muscular VSDs, for example, frequently become smaller or close naturally. Other types of VSD may behave differently and require continued observation.

This is why follow-up is important. Rather than looking only at the initial size of the defect, we monitor how the VSD and the child's heart change as the child grows.

Parent: How do you know how serious the VSD is?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

An echocardiogram (2D Echo with Doppler) is the key investigation for evaluating a VSD.

It helps us assess:

  • The exact location and type of VSD
  • The size of the defect
  • The direction and velocity of blood flow through it
  • Whether the VSD is restrictive or non-restrictive
  • Enlargement of the heart chambers due to excess blood flow
  • Estimated pulmonary artery pressure
  • The function of the heart
  • The heart valves, particularly the aortic valve in certain types of VSD
  • Any associated congenital heart abnormalities

The decision about treatment is therefore not based simply on the measured diameter of the “hole.” We look at what the VSD is actually doing to the baby’s heart and circulation.

Parent: Does every child with VSD need surgery?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

No.

A small VSD that is not causing significant heart enlargement, pulmonary hypertension or other complications may require only periodic follow-up.

A significant VSD may require treatment when it causes problems such as heart-failure symptoms, poor weight gain, significant enlargement of the left-sided heart chambers, excessive blood flow to the lungs or elevated pulmonary artery pressure.

Certain VSDs may also require closure if they cause complications such as aortic valve prolapse or aortic valve leakage, even when the child does not have severe symptoms.

Treatment depends on the individual child and the anatomy of the VSD.

Medicines may be used to control symptoms of heart failure and help the baby feed and grow better. However, medicines do not close the VSD itself.

When closure is required, treatment may involve surgical closure or, in carefully selected anatomically suitable VSDs, catheter-based device closure.

The best approach depends on the child's age and weight, the type and location of the VSD, its relationship to the heart valves and the overall clinical situation.

Parent: If my baby has a large VSD, can we simply wait for it to close naturally?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Not always.

It is reasonable to observe many small VSDs because they may become smaller or close spontaneously.

But a large VSD causing significant symptoms, poor growth, heart enlargement or high pulmonary artery pressure should not simply be observed indefinitely in the hope that it will close.

If excessive blood flow to the lungs continues for a prolonged period, it can eventually damage the pulmonary blood vessels.

The purpose of regular follow-up is therefore not only to see whether the VSD becomes smaller, but also to identify the right time for intervention if it becomes necessary.

Parent: Can my child live a normal life with or after a VSD?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

In most cases, the outlook is very good.

Children with small, uncomplicated VSDs generally grow normally and can lead active lives.

Children who require closure of a significant VSD can also usually do extremely well after appropriate treatment and follow-up.

The long-term outlook depends on the individual anatomy and whether there are associated heart abnormalities or complications.

This is why I advise parents not to compare their child's VSD with another child's “hole in the heart.” The same diagnosis can have very different implications in different children.

What is the most important thing you want parents to remember?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Please don’t be frightened simply by the words “hole in the heart.”

Instead, ask:

  • How big and restrictive is the VSD?
  • Where is it located?
  • Is it causing enlargement of the heart?
  • What is the estimated pressure in the lung circulation?
  • Is my baby feeding and growing properly?
  • Is there any effect on the heart valves?
  • Does my child need treatment, or only regular follow-up?

A VSD can mean very different things in different children.

Some VSDs need nothing more than observation. Some close naturally. Others require treatment at the appropriate time.

With careful pediatric cardiology assessment and regular follow-up, we can usually give parents a clear understanding of what the VSD means for their child and, when necessary, choose the safest and most appropriate time for treatment.

Blue Baby: Why Does a Baby Turn Blue and Is It Always a Heart Problem?

Written by Dr. Sushil Shukla, Pediatric Cardiologist

Seeing a baby's lips or tongue turn blue can be an extremely frightening experience for parents.

In my practice as a Pediatric Cardiologist, parents often hear terms such as "blue baby," "cyanosis," or "Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)" and immediately fear the worst.

Why does a baby become blue? Does every blue baby have a heart defect? How urgently does the baby need treatment? Can children with these heart conditions grow up normally?

Below, I answer the most common questions parents ask me about cyanosis in newborns, congenital heart disease, and treatment options.

Q1: Doctor, what does "blue baby" actually mean?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

The medical term for bluish discoloration is cyanosis. It occurs when the blood circulating through the body contains less oxygen than normal.

In a baby with true central cyanosis, we notice a bluish or dusky appearance particularly involving the tongue, lips, and inside of the mouth.

However, bluish hands and feet alone (peripheral cyanosis)—especially in a newborn—do not necessarily mean that the baby has a heart defect. The important question is whether the baby has true central cyanosis and a low oxygen saturation.

Q2: Does a blue baby always have heart disease?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

No. Low oxygen levels in a newborn or child can have several causes, including problems involving the heart, lungs, or circulation, and occasionally other medical conditions.

However, congenital heart disease (CHD) is an important cause of persistent or unexplained cyanosis in babies and requires prompt assessment. This is why persistent bluish discoloration of the tongue or lips should never simply be ignored.

Q3: What heart conditions can cause a baby to become blue?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Several congenital heart defects can cause cyanosis. One of the best-known is Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF).

Other important causes include:

  • Transposition of the great arteries
  • Pulmonary atresia
  • Tricuspid atresia
  • Single-ventricle heart disease
  • Complex congenital heart defects

Some of these conditions present immediately after birth, while others may become more obvious over the following weeks or months.

Q4: What exactly is Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Tetralogy of Fallot is a congenital heart condition in which several abnormalities occur together. The two most important functional problems are:

  1. Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD): An opening between the two lower chambers of the heart.
  2. Pulmonary Obstruction: Obstruction to blood flow from the right ventricle towards the lungs.

Because blood has difficulty reaching the lungs, some oxygen-poor blood can pass through the VSD and enter the body's main circulation. This reduces oxygen levels and causes cyanosis. The severity of TOF varies considerably depending on how severe the obstruction to blood flow towards the lungs is.

Q5: Are all babies with TOF blue from birth?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

No. Some babies with TOF may have obvious cyanosis soon after birth, while others may initially have relatively normal oxygen levels and appear only mildly blue—or not visibly blue at all.

The amount of cyanosis largely depends on the degree of obstruction to pulmonary blood flow. Therefore, the severity of TOF cannot be judged simply by looking at how blue the baby appears.

Q6: What symptoms should parents watch for?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Depending on the severity of the condition, parents may notice:

  • Bluish discoloration of the tongue or lips
  • Low oxygen saturation levels
  • Fast or difficult breathing
  • Difficulty feeding or tiring during feeds
  • Poor weight gain
  • Irritability
  • Episodes where the child suddenly becomes much more blue
  • Fainting or becoming unusually floppy during a severe episode
  • Reduced exercise tolerance in older children

Any baby with significant persistent cyanosis, breathing difficulty, poor responsiveness, or a sudden worsening in color requires urgent medical assessment.

Q7: Sometimes my child suddenly becomes very blue and starts crying or breathing rapidly. What could this be?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

In some children with TOF, there can be sudden episodes of worsening cyanosis known as hypercyanotic spells or "Tet spells."

During a spell, the child may suddenly become much more blue, breathe rapidly, and become extremely irritable. Severe episodes can cause weakness, loss of consciousness, or, rarely, seizures.

A suspected hypercyanotic spell requires urgent medical attention. Parents of a child known to be at risk should receive an individual emergency plan from their pediatric cardiology team.

Q8: Why do some older children with TOF squat after running or playing?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Squatting is a classic observation in some untreated children with TOF. A child may instinctively squat after physical activity because this position changes the circulation in a way that temporarily improves blood flow to the lungs and increases oxygen levels.

Today, because TOF is diagnosed and treated much earlier, this classic presentation is seen less commonly than in the past.

Q9: How is a blue baby investigated?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

The first steps include a careful examination and measurement of the baby's oxygen saturation.

An echocardiogram (2D Echo with Doppler) is the key investigation when congenital heart disease is suspected. It allows us to examine the heart's structure, blood flow, pulmonary arteries, and associated abnormalities.

Depending on the condition, additional tests like an ECG, chest X-ray, CT scan, cardiac MRI, or cardiac catheterization may occasionally be required.

Q10: Does every baby with TOF need surgery?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

TOF generally requires definitive treatment. For most children, the aim is complete surgical repair, which involves closing the VSD and relieving the obstruction to blood flow from the right ventricle to the lungs.

The appropriate timing depends on the baby's anatomy, oxygen level, symptoms, pulmonary artery development, weight, and overall clinical condition. Some babies—particularly those who are severely cyanotic or have complex anatomy—may require an earlier procedure (a catheter-based intervention or surgical palliative procedure) to improve pulmonary blood flow before complete repair.

Q11: Can TOF be treated without open-heart surgery?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

For most children with classical TOF, complete repair is a surgical procedure.

However, catheter-based procedures can play an important role in selected babies. In certain high-risk or very small infants, catheter interventions may be used to improve blood flow to the lungs and allow the child to grow before definitive repair.

Q12: Can a child live a normal life after TOF repair?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

The outlook for children with TOF has improved dramatically. Most children who undergo successful repair can grow, attend school, and participate in normal daily activities.

However, children who have undergone TOF repair require lifelong cardiology follow-up. Some may later develop problems such as pulmonary valve leakage, residual obstruction, enlargement of the right ventricle, or heart-rhythm abnormalities, which may require further care. Regular follow-up allows us to identify these changes early.

Q13: What should I do if my baby's oxygen level is low?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

A persistently low oxygen saturation in a baby should be medically evaluated rather than interpreted in isolation.

If a baby becomes suddenly much more blue, has significant breathing difficulty, becomes unusually sleepy or unresponsive, collapses, or has a seizure, emergency medical attention should be sought immediately.

Q14: What is the most important message for parents of a "blue baby"?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

First, don't assume that every bluish discoloration means serious heart disease—but persistent central cyanosis should never be ignored.

Conditions such as Tetralogy of Fallot are serious congenital heart defects, but they are also highly treatable. With timely diagnosis, appropriate intervention, and long-term pediatric cardiology follow-up, the majority of children with repaired TOF can have a very good quality of life.
