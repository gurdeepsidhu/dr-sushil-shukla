Understanding Congenital Heart Defects in Newborns

Written by Dr. Sushil Shukla • Dr. Sushil Shukla

Congenital Heart Defects (CHD) are structural heart issues present at birth. They can range from simple "holes in the heart" (like Atrial Septal Defects or Patent Ductus Arteriosus) to complex structural anomalies that restrict blood flow to the lungs or body.

Early identification through fetal echocardiography during pregnancy or clinical evaluation immediately after birth is vital. Today, advanced transcatheter interventions allow us to close defects like ASDs and PDAs using custom closing devices threaded through blood vessels, completely avoiding the need for open-heart surgery.

Heart Murmur in Children: Parents Ask, Dr. Sushil Shukla Answers

Written by Dr. Sushil Shukla, Pediatric Cardiologist

When parents are told that their child has a “heart murmur,” the first reaction is often worry. But a heart murmur does not necessarily mean that something is wrong with the heart.

To understand what a heart murmur really means, we asked Dr. Sushil Shukla, Pediatric Cardiologist, some of the most common questions parents have.

Parent: Doctor, what exactly is a heart murmur?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

A heart murmur is an additional sound heard when a doctor listens to the heart with a stethoscope.

Importantly, a heart murmur is a finding, not a diagnosis of heart disease.

Many healthy children have what we call an innocent or functional murmur. These murmurs are produced by normal blood flow through a structurally normal heart and are particularly common during childhood.

However, a murmur can sometimes be caused by a structural or other heart problem. Therefore, we assess the murmur together with the child's symptoms, examination findings and overall health.

Parent: Does every child with a murmur have a hole in the heart?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

No, absolutely not.

This is one of the most common misconceptions among parents.

A murmur may be present even when the heart is completely normal. Conversely, congenital heart conditions—including holes in the heart, valve abnormalities and other structural problems—can also produce murmurs.

So, hearing a murmur is a reason for appropriate assessment, not a reason to panic.

Parent: How do you decide whether a murmur is innocent or abnormal?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

We look at much more than just the presence of a murmur.

I consider the child's age, symptoms, growth, oxygen saturation, pulses and blood pressure, along with the location, intensity, timing and other characteristics of the murmur.

In many children, the history and clinical examination are reassuring enough to identify a typical innocent murmur.

If there is any concern about an underlying heart condition, further evaluation—particularly an echocardiogram (Echo)—may be recommended. An ECG may also be useful in selected situations.

Parent: What symptoms should parents watch for?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Most children with innocent murmurs have no symptoms at all.

However, a child with a murmur should be evaluated carefully if there are symptoms such as:

  • Breathlessness or unusually fast breathing
  • Difficulty feeding or excessive sweating during feeds, particularly in infants
  • Poor weight gain or poor growth
  • Bluish discoloration of the lips, tongue or inside of the mouth
  • Unusual tiredness
  • Reduced ability to exercise or difficulty keeping up with other children
  • Palpitations
  • Chest pain during physical activity
  • Dizziness or fainting, particularly during exercise

These symptoms do not automatically mean that a child has heart disease, but they deserve proper medical assessment.

Parent: Does every child with a heart murmur need an Echo?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

Not necessarily.

An echocardiogram is an extremely useful test, but every innocent-sounding murmur does not automatically require one.

A pediatric cardiologist can first assess the child's history and perform a detailed cardiovascular examination. Based on these findings, we can decide whether an Echo is necessary.

When required, an echocardiogram is a safe, painless ultrasound examination that allows us to assess the heart chambers, valves, major blood vessels and blood flow without radiation.

Parent: My child is otherwise completely normal. Should I still be worried?

Dr. Sushil Shukla:

If your child is active, growing normally and has no concerning symptoms, there is usually no reason to become frightened simply because a murmur has been heard.

In fact, innocent murmurs are very common in otherwise healthy children.

The important step is to determine whether the murmur has the characteristics of an innocent murmur or whether further evaluation is required.

When should a murmur receive prompt attention?

A murmur deserves prompt medical assessment when it is associated with bluish discoloration, low oxygen saturation, significant breathing difficulty, poor feeding or growth, abnormal pulses, exertional chest pain, fainting during exercise, or significant exercise intolerance.

A murmur detected in a newborn or young infant also deserves particularly careful assessment.

Dr. Sushil Shukla's Message to Parents

A heart murmur is a finding, not a diagnosis.

Many heart murmurs heard during childhood are completely innocent and occur in children with structurally normal hearts. However, a murmur can occasionally be the first clue to congenital or acquired heart disease.

My advice to parents is simple:

Don't panic—but don't ignore a persistent or unexplained heart murmur either.

A careful pediatric cardiac evaluation can usually determine whether the murmur is innocent or whether further investigation is required, giving families the clarity and reassurance they need.
